\section*{Setup}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Document:} \emph{Magic: The Gathering Comprehensive Rules} (June 2026, 311 pages). I kept only the numbered rules and split them into 971 chunks (one rule + its sub-rules + section heading; median 187 tokens).
  \item \textbf{Models:} Bi-Encoder \code{bge-small-en-v1.5} + FAISS exact cosine search; Cross-Encoder \code{ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2}; LLM \code{gemini-3.5-flash-lite} (temperature 0, same prompt for both pipelines, told to answer only from the context).
  \item \textbf{Queries:} I wrote 49 player-style questions: negation (14), exception (9), keyword trap (10), buried rule (6), control (10). I fixed the gold rule for each one before running retrieval.
\end{itemize}

\section{Architecture comparison}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Bi-Encoder:} encodes the question and each chunk \emph{separately} into one vector; relevance is their cosine similarity. Chunk vectors are computed once and indexed, so search is fast. But small words like ``can't'' or ``except'' get blurred inside one vector.
  \item \textbf{Cross-Encoder:} reads the question and a chunk \emph{together}; self-attention runs across both texts, so it catches negations and conditions. Nothing can be precomputed: every pair needs its own forward pass.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Pipeline 1:} Question \ra{} Bi-Encoder + FAISS (top 10) \ra{} top 3 \ra{} Gemini \ra{} Answer~1.\\
\textbf{Pipeline 2:} Question \ra{} Bi-Encoder + FAISS (top 10) \ra{} Cross-Encoder re-scores the same 10 \ra{} new top 3 \ra{} Gemini \ra{} Answer~2.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Why the baseline missed:} it favours chunks that share words with the question. Q1's top 3 were general attack rules; ``defender can't attack'' was \#5. Q27 asks about a token that ``dies'' in the ``graveyard'', but the rule says it ``ceases to exist'' (\#10 \ra{} \#1).
  \item \textbf{Where re-ranking hurt:} Q46 and Q49. The MS MARCO-trained re-ranker preferred an on-topic but less precise rule (e.g.\ 608.1 over 405.5 for stack order).
  \item \textbf{Not in top 10} (Q24, Q26, Q28, Q30, Q41, Q47): when I ran the Cross-Encoder over all 971 chunks, it found Q26, Q41 at \#1 and Q24 at \#3, so those misses come from the Bi-Encoder. Q28, Q30, Q47 are missed by both models.
\end{itemize}

\section{Scaling question}
A Cross-Encoder's score depends on the question, so it cannot be precomputed. Scoring every chunk means $N$ forward passes per query. The cost I measured is linear at $\approx$49\,ms per pair (rows beyond 971 are extrapolated).

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{rrr}
\toprule
Chunks & Cross-Encoder over all chunks & Two-stage (Bi-Encoder + re-rank 10) \\
\midrule
971 (measured) & 48 s & 0.71 s \\
100,000 & 4,924 s (1.4 hours) & 0.72 s \\
1,000,000 & 49,244 s (13.7 hours) & 0.85 s \\
10,000,000 & 492,444 s (5.7 days) & 2.09 s \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
  \item The Bi-Encoder does its heavy work once (indexing); each query is one encoding + a fast search.
  \item So I use two stages: the Bi-Encoder narrows millions of chunks to 10, and the Cross-Encoder only orders those.
  \item Trade-off: a recall ceiling. If stage one misses the gold chunk (Q24, Q26, Q41), stage two can't recover it.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Limitations:} I wrote and judged all 49 questions myself on one document; I corrected the gold lists for Q40 and Q49 after reading the answers; large-scale timings are extrapolated.

\section*{Conclusion}
Re-ranking raised Hit@3 from 0.67 to 0.84 and correct answers from 6 to 14 (of 23), mostly on negation, exception and keyword-trap questions. It costs about 0.7\,s per query on CPU, occasionally demotes the most precise rule, and cannot recover chunks missed by the first stage. The two-stage design gives the best balance of speed and accuracy.
